How decisions are made

Laya answers 6 questions per alert in one pass (category, customer impact, self-recovering, sustained, business risk, severity). oncall_gate/policy.py applies auditable rules: maintenance → suppress; tier-1 txn failures → page; recovered flaps → suppress; sustained but no impact → watch; same-service alerts in 10 min merge into one incident.

Laya vs Ollama: same alerts, same six questions, same policy rules

Only the engine changes. Laya answers in one forward pass; the local Ollama LLM generates JSON text. Both feed the identical rule table (oncall_gate/policy.py) and incident correlation, so differences in speed, accuracy and pages come from the engine alone. Ollama runs on this machine too (ollama serve).

Architecture: how one alert becomes a page / ticket / suppress decision
What happens when you press Replay
  1. The browser sends each of the 24 alerts, one at a time, to POST /api/alert (app.py). Live mode just adds a delay between them.
  2. render_state() packs service, tier and the alert text into the short text Laya will read.
  3. Laya reads that text once and answers six questions in a single forward pass: category, customer impact, self-recovering, sustained, business risk, severity. It returns probabilities, never free text.
  4. policy.decide() applies fixed rules to those probabilities (e.g. maintenance window → suppress; tier-1 transaction failures → page). Thresholds live in code so they can be audited and changed without retraining.
  5. OnCallState correlates: alerts on the same service within 10 minutes merge into one incident, a second “watch” signal escalates to a page, and a page is written to the simulated pager.
  6. The UI shows the decision and why, updates the pager log, and the scorecard compares every decision with the hidden ground-truth label in alerts.py.

Why split “model” from “policy”?

Laya is good at reading messy text and judging it (“is this transient?”, “are customers affected?”). It is not a good place to hide business rules. So the model supplies probabilities and the policy decides what to do with them. A missed page is costlier than an extra page, so the rules lean toward paging on tier-1 services, and a hard rule (maintenance window) can never be overridden by the model.
